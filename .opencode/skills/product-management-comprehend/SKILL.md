# Comprehend the situation

**C** — Comprehend the Situation. This is the first step of CIRCLES, and its whole job is to prevent
solving the wrong problem. Everything after this letter is downstream of what you settle here, so
what you skip here you pay for seven steps later.

## Why the step exists

Each letter of CIRCLES was written against a specific failure, and this one is against **solving the
wrong problem**. A product manager who rushes to a solution without establishing the situation ends
up with a need that is half-met or a solution that only partly solves it — the outcome is not wrong,
it is *unasked-for*.

It is a step with a cost: time spent here is time not spent listing options. That trade is worth it
because a wrong problem is not recoverable downstream at any price.

## What to establish

Three things. The method's own guidance is to clarify the goal, understand the constraints, and
understand the context.

### 1. The goal

What is this change meant to achieve? The same feature request can serve opposite goals, and the
answer changes what you build.

- Is this aimed at **revenue**, at **engagement**, at **retention**, at **cost**, at **delight**?
- Once the goal is fixed, the **metric that shows success** becomes knowable — and it is knowable
  only after the goal is fixed. Engagement as the goal makes time-on-site a candidate metric;
  revenue as the goal makes something else the candidate.
- Is this **greenfield**, or an **improvement of something that exists**? This changes the whole
  approach, because an improvement is bounded by what already shipped and greenfield is not.

Ask for this if it was not given. Do not infer the goal from the shape of the request.

### 2. The constraints

Resources, budget, and timescale. These do not narrow the answer afterwards — they shape which
answer is reachable, and they are cheapest to learn before the options are on the table.

Also in scope: what the product must keep doing. An improvement that breaks something else is not
an improvement.

### 3. The context

The 5 W's and H, as the method's own gloss puts it:

- **What** is it?
- **Who** is it for?
- **Why** do they need it?
- **When** is it available, and against what deadline?
- **Where** is it available — which surface, which part of the product?
- **How** does it work today?

Context includes the world around the product: what else the customer uses for this, and what else
is competing for the same job.

## Ask, or state your assumption

If the question is genuinely under-specified, **ask one clarifying question** — and one is the
number that works. This step is not an interrogation.

A good clarifying question is one whose answer **changes your answer**. It narrows scope, surfaces a
business goal, or exposes a constraint.

> "Are we trying to grow this to new users or deepen value for existing ones?"
> "Is this greenfield, or an improvement of what exists?"

Both change the entire approach that follows.

A bad clarifying question is one whose answer is already in the question. "Who is the target user?"
asked of a prompt that already says *for commuters* is theatrical — it looks like rigour and buys
nothing. That is the failure mode this step is most often faked in, and faking it is worse than
skipping it.

**If you cannot ask** — nobody is available, or the question is yours alone to answer — then you
**state your assumption and carry it forward**. An unstated assumption is the thing that quietly
invalidates the whole analysis six steps later. State it, then proceed.

## What this step produces

- The goal, and the metric it implies
- The constraints that bound the answer
- The context, including how the product works today
- Every assumption you had to make instead of an answer, **named as assumptions**

Carry all of it forward. The next step is the customer, and it depends on what you settled here.

## What this step is not

- **Not identifying the customer.** That is the next letter.
- **Not the problem itself.** You have established the situation, not yet the need.
- **Not a questionnaire.** One question, or a stated assumption, then move on.

## Standing rules for this step

1. **Establish the goal before anything else.** Everything downstream depends on it.
2. **One clarifying question, and it must change the answer.** A question whose answer is already
   given is theatre.
3. **Never let an assumption pass unstated.** Write it down and carry it.
4. **Do not skip to a solution here.** That is the exact failure this letter exists to prevent.
