# Identify the customer

**I** — Identify the customer and the stakeholders. This step exists so that a need is never claimed
about nobody. A need asserted about "users" is not yet a need; it is a guess about an average of
people who do not exist.

## Why the step exists

Each letter of CIRCLES is written against a specific failure. This one is against **missing critical
stakeholders** — and, underneath that, against picking a cohort so vague that nothing you build for
it can be wrong.

The method commits to **a single customer segment**, deliberately. One cohort, defended, beats a
broad stroke that quietly answers to everyone and convinces no one.

## Segmented, not described

A weak cohort is a **demographic label with no behaviour attached**. "Adults 25–35 who are busy" is
unfalsifiable — you cannot design for it, and no evidence can ever refute it.

A strong cohort is defined by **context and friction**:

- **Context** — the situation they are in when they hit this. What are they doing, with what, at
  what moment?
- **Friction** — what goes wrong, and what it costs them. Where does the current path break?

The method's own illustration of the failure mode is cooking the wrong dish: do not cook beef
Wellington for vegans. A cohort is a specific set of people in a specific situation, not a
demographic.

### Segment, then choose, then say why

1. **Segment** the user base — the distinct groups that use this product differently.
2. **Choose one** to design for.
3. **Say why that one.** The reasoning is the deliverable. "This cohort, because their friction is
   the most acute and they are the largest affected group" is the answer. A cohort without a reason
   is a preference.

## Stakeholders, and the other side of the market

Every product has stakeholders who are not the user. Name the ones the work actually affects.

**Two-sided and multi-sided platforms need an extra step.** If you are working on a platform, you
must:

- **State which side you are designing for**, and why that side.
- **Say that you know the other side exists**, and may be affected.

Leaving the other side unmentioned is the failure here — not choosing it, but not acknowledging it.
A marketplace change that helps buyers and silently harms sellers has been decided without the
seller ever entering the analysis.

For internal tools, "the customer" may be an internal team, and the stakeholders are whoever absorbs
the work it displaces.

## This is not a one-time act

Identifying the customer is not only a step at the start. The method treats it as something that
holds across the product's lifecycle, not a single upfront act: who the customer is and which cohort
matters can shift as the product and its usage change.

## What this step produces

- **One cohort**, defined by context and friction — not by demographics alone
- **The reasoning** for choosing it
- **The stakeholders** affected, including any other side of the market
- For a platform: **which side you are designing for**, and an acknowledgement of the other

Carry it forward. The next step reports a need, and this is who the need belongs to.

## What this step is not

- **Not the need itself.** Naming the pain is the next letter.
- **Not a persona document.** A cohort is a working choice, defended in a sentence or two.
- **Not everyone.** Choosing one cohort is the point.

## Standing rules for this step

1. **One cohort, chosen on purpose, with the reasoning stated.** Two cohorts is a decision deferred.
2. **Context and friction, not demographics.** If you cannot picture the situation, the cohort is not
   yet real.
3. **On a platform, name the side and acknowledge the other.** Silence about the other side is a
   decision already taken.
4. **No need is claimed about an unnamed cohort.** That is the next step's contract with this one.
