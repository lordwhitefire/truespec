# Cut through prioritization

**C** — Cut. The second C. This step exists to stop you trying to solve everything — and it is the
step most often performed as a gesture rather than a decision.

## Why the step exists

Each letter of CIRCLES is written against a specific failure. This one is against **trying to solve
everything**. Every need reported is real and urgent to whoever reported it; the method's premise is
that you cannot serve all of them, so something must be chosen and something must be refused.

The discipline is not only in choosing. **It is in refusing out loud.** A cut that is not stated as
a cut reads as a list where everything still matters.

## Do it out loud, with a rationale

Two requirements, and both matter:

1. **Out loud** — the cut is part of the reasoning, not a private judgement. "I am choosing this
   one, and here is what I am not doing."
2. **With a rationale** — the reasoning, not just the verdict.

A ranking nobody can follow is not a decision; it is a preference wearing a decision's clothes.

## The axes

The method's own scaffold is three axes:

1. **Frequency of the pain** — how often does the customer hit this?
2. **Acuteness** — how much does it cost them when it happens?
3. **Alignment with the business goal** — the goal you fixed in Comprehend. This is why that step
   is not optional: without a goal there is no third axis, and the cut has nothing to align to.

Say which need scores highest **and why**. High-scoring on one axis does not settle it — a need
that is frequent but trivially cheap may lose to a rare, expensive, highly aligned one, and the
reasoning is the deliverable.

## Scoring frameworks, when one is wanted

Prioritization frameworks plug into this step; none of them replaces the decision. Where the
knowledge base carries them, they are:

- **RICE** — Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort. From Intercom. Reach in people per period, from
  real product metrics where possible. Impact on a scale (3 massive, 2 high, 1 medium, 0.5 low, 0.25
  minimal). Confidence as your actual confidence (100% high, 80% medium, 50% low, below that a
  moonshot). Effort in person-months, kept rough — whole numbers, or 0.5 for well under a month.
- **MoSCoW** — Must / Should / Could / Won't.
- **Weighted impact scoring** — criteria (demand, cost, feasibility) against weights, totalled.

### What RICE is for, and what it is not

RICE measures **impact per unit of time worked**. Its own origin says the scores should **not be a
hard and fast rule**, and gives two honest reasons to work a lower-scored item first: it is a
dependency for something else, or it is table stakes for selling to certain customers.

**A score is an input to the cut, not the cut.** Two projects tied on score are still one choice.
Overriding a score is legitimate; overriding it **silently** is not.

Its original article names four specific ways prioritization goes wrong, worth naming because each is
a thing you can catch yourself doing: it is satisfying to work on **pet ideas you would use
yourself** instead of broad-reach work; tempting to favour **clever** ideas over ones that hit goals
directly; easy to double down on what you are **already confident about**; and easy to **discount the
effort** one project needs relative to another. Each is a bias, not an error, which is why the step
asks for a rationale.

## What this step produces

- **The needs ranked**, on the axes, with the axes named
- **One need chosen**, and the reasoning that chose it
- **What was cut, stated explicitly** — the discarded needs, by name
- **The framework used, if any**, and any case where its answer was overridden, with the reason

Carry it forward. The next step lists solutions to the chosen need, and only to it.

## What this step is not

- **Not a scoring exercise performed for its own sake.** A score that changes nothing was theatre.
- **Not a rejection of the other needs.** They are real; they are not this quarter's.
- **Not the solution.** Nothing has been built or chosen yet.

## Standing rules for this step

1. **State the cut.** A prioritization that is not said out loud leaves every need looking equal.
2. **Name the axes before ranking on them.** An unnamed axis cannot be argued with.
3. **Align to the goal you fixed in Comprehend.** Without it, there is no third axis.
4. **A score may be overridden; say so and say why.** Silently ignoring your own framework is the
   thing this step exists to prevent.
