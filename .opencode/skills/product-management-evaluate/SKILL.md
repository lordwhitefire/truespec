# Evaluate trade-offs

**E** — Evaluate trade-offs. No option is free. This step exists to stop an impressive option from
carrying itself, and to make the price of each one visible.

## Why the step exists

Each letter of CIRCLES is written against a specific failure. This one is against **choosing without
weighing**: unless the trade-off analysis is actually unbiased, the most impressive option will
simply win, and it will have won on presentation rather than on merit.

The failure is specific — **an unbalanced trade-off analysis is how a good option wins a bad
comparison.** So the analysis is done deliberately, on axes fixed before the options are read, and
the drawbacks are written down for every option including the one you favour.

## Where the weight has moved

This step has changed more than the others, and the change matters.

The framework was designed when **"can we build it?" was a genuine gating question.** That question
has largely stopped being a differentiator. Feasibility is now the **floor** — a checkbox, not a
discriminator — and the evaluative weight sits on two other questions:

1. **Viable** — is this a problem people or companies will pay to have solved, at scale?
2. **Lovable** — does this fit where users already work, without asking them to form a new habit?

An analysis that spends its time on engineering difficulty and ten seconds on those two has done the
step backwards.

### AI-era trade-offs, which belong in the same analysis

Where the option involves a probabilistic or AI-driven behaviour, three things join viable and
lovable rather than replacing them:

- **Will people use it, or does it add a task to an already overloaded workflow?**
- **Does the output need to be editable?** What does a user do when the system is wrong?
- **Cognitive offloading** — the tool should reduce decisions, not add them. A feature that makes the
  user adjudicate machine output has moved the work, not removed it.
- **Error consequences.** Who absorbs a wrong answer, and how badly?

### The other axes, when they are the ones that matter

- **Cost**, and time to implement
- **Potential user impact** — who is helped, who is hurt
- **Risk against reward** — including what fails and how visibly
- **Alignment with business strategy** — not only with the goal from Comprehend

A **trade-off matrix** (cost, complexity, time, potential impact) is a serviceable instrument. So are
plain pros and cons. The instrument matters less than writing the drawbacks down.

## What a real evaluation produces

- **Every option scored against the same axes**, fixed before reading the options
- **The drawbacks of each**, including your preferred option — a favourable option with no stated
  drawback has not been evaluated
- **Short- and long-term consequences** of each, which is where options separate most sharply
- **The option whose drawbacks do not imply irresponsible risk or work against strategy**

"That no solution is free" is the premise. **An option with no material drawback is either
under-evaluated or wrong**, and both are worth correcting before the next step.

## What this step produces

- **A weighed comparison**, on axes named in advance
- **The risk called out** for the option you favour
- **The trade-off itself named** — what choosing this costs, stated in terms of what is given up

Carry it forward. The next step closes with one recommendation, and it must be a recommendation you
have actually paid for.

## What this step is not

- **Not a feasibility review.** Buildability is a checkbox that has already passed.
- **Not a defence of one option.** Bias is the failure this step is built around.
- **Not the recommendation.** You have not chosen yet; you have priced each option.

## Standing rules for this step

1. **Fix the axes before you read the options.** Otherwise the axes follow your preference.
2. **Write the drawbacks of the option you favour.** No drawbacks listed means under-evaluated.
3. **Feasibility is the floor, not the test.** Spend the time on viable and lovable.
4. **Where the option is AI-shaped, ask who absorbs a wrong answer.** That is a trade-off, and the
   most consequential one.
