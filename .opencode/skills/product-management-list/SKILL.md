# List solutions

**L** — List solutions. This step exists to prevent **obvious or boring answers** — the first idea,
the familiar pattern, and eight variations of it.

## Why the step exists

Each letter of CIRCLES is written against a specific failure. This one is against obviousness. A
list of eight variations of one idea is not breadth; it is one idea with better odds of not being
read.

And there is a second failure, the mirror image: **you can also list too few.** The method's original
suggestion was 10+ ideas, on the reasoning that more ideas make a great one more likely. Read that
as a floor on genuine diversity, not a quota — but know that the failure being guarded against is
*one idea*, whatever the count says.

## Two or three, meaningfully different

Where the method's own worked guidance has settled on practice, it asks for **two or three
meaningfully different options**, not eight incremental variations. A useful spectrum:

1. **A quick win, narrow scope** — small, safe, fast. Buys learning or buys goodwill.
2. **A core bet** — addresses the need directly, and is the option that actually resolves it.
3. **A longer-horizon idea** — worth naming even if you do not pick it, because it shows what the
   real shape of the problem is.

The spectrum matters more than the count. Three options that sit at three points on it give the
next step something real to weigh. Eight options that differ only in size do not.

**Judge each against the need, not against the pitch.** An option that solves the reported need is in
scope. An option that happens to be interesting, or that the customer asked for by name, is not — the
want and the need were separated one step ago.

## Breadth beats polish

Generate more than you keep. The purpose of the step is to widen the space before the next step
narrows it — evaluating three weak options because only three were written is a self-inflicted
constraint, and it is invisible if you do not notice it.

**One idea is a failure of this step even when it is a good idea.** A strong first idea is still
one option, and it is the one most likely to be anchored on by everything that follows.

## Some structures that help

Where the need is ambiguous, the options are usually more distinct if you vary the **kind** of
solution rather than its details:

- **Change the surface** — solve it where the work already happens, rather than in a new place.
- **Change the trigger** — the same capability, delivered at a different moment.
- **Change who does it** — remove the customer's work rather than making it easier.
- **Change the default** — the smallest change of all, and sometimes the whole answer.

These are heuristics for divergence, not a checklist to satisfy. Their value is that they produce
options that are not siblings.

## What this step produces

- **Two or three options, genuinely different from each other**, spanning quick win, core bet, and
  longer horizon
- **Each traced to the chosen need** — why it addresses that need, not the original feature want
- **Enough discarded ideas to show the space was widened**, if you generated more than you kept

Carry it forward. The next step weighs them, and it cannot weigh three that are really one.

## What this step is not

- **Not the decision.** Choosing happens next.
- **Not a pitch.** Options are stated, not sold. Selling biases the weighing.
- **Not variations of one idea.** If two options would lose the same argument, they are one option.
- **Not a feature list.** These are solutions to the need you cut to.

## Standing rules for this step

1. **Two or three, and they must differ in kind.** Siblings are one option.
2. **Span the spectrum.** A quick win, a core bet, and a horizon option.
3. **Answer the need you cut to**, not the want the customer named.
4. **One option is a failure here**, however good it is.
